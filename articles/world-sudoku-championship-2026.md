# Kolkata, from the couch

[Ming Letian of China won the 19th World Sudoku Championship](https://wspc2026.logicmastersindia.com/?g=MeetTheChampions). I was not in the room. I was on the couch, which is my usual venue, and then I went and read the paperwork.

The sudoku half ran in Kolkata from October 11 to October 15, 2026. Competition was October 12 and 13, with the awards on the 13th. The [championship page](https://wspc2026.logicmastersindia.com/?g=WSC) calls those five days the event: arrival and an opening on the 11th, two days of rounds, sightseeing on the 14th, departure on the 15th. [Logic Masters India](https://wspc2026.logicmastersindia.com/) hosted it at The Westin Kolkata Rajarhat. The same building then held the World Puzzle Championship through October 17. Different contest. [Ken Endo of Japan won that one](https://wspc2026.logicmastersindia.com/?g=MeetTheChampions). This piece is the sudoku half.

The first World Sudoku Championship was Lucca in 2006. That story is in the [history piece](/articles/history-of-sudoku/). This was the 19th.

## Who won

The organizers' [champions list](https://wspc2026.logicmastersindia.com/?g=MeetTheChampions) puts Ming Letian, China, on the 2026 line, and China as the winning team. The same list has him winning in Beijing in 2024. The 2025 champion, in Eger, was Dai Tantan, also of China. So this is Ming's second individual title. He was not the defender. China has the team title for 2024, 2025, and 2026, three in a row.

The [live results page](https://wspc2026.logicmastersindia.com/?g=WSCResults) was returning a server error when I loaded it on October 23. The scores below are the ones posted while that table was up.

The name is Dai Tantan on the champions list and Tantan Dai on the [participant list](https://wspc2026.logicmastersindia.com/?g=WSCParticipants). Same solver.

On teams, the UK post has CHN-A on 13,089, USA-A on 11,058, and JPN-A on 10,322. It also says the organizers did not rank unofficial teams, and that one of those sides, UN-07, scored 11,057. One point behind the USA, and off the medal list on purpose.

The participant list is the roster I can check. China's A-team names there are Huang Mingrui, marked captain and A-team, plus Hu Yuxuan and Ming Letian. Kota Morinishi's third place is a strange sentence until you read the old lines. The champions list has him winning the individual title in 2014, 2015, 2017, and 2018. Four titles, and third in Kolkata.

The WSC page says 157 confirmed participants from 32 countries. The participant table is the longer document, because it also lists guests and captains who are not playing. I counted 32 countries in that table, which matches the page.

## What they were solving

The [final instruction booklet](https://logicmastersindia.com/lmitests/dl.asp?attachmentid=1249) is dated September 30, 2026. The rounds are named for the Mahabharata. Each one gets a short scene, then the rules. I am not going to retell the epic. The scoring is the part that changes how you read a result.

There are 10 individual rounds, 5 team rounds, and a playoff. October 12 is rounds 1 through 9. October 13 is rounds 10 through 15, then the playoff at 17:00. A round is a stack of puzzles in a printed booklet, a time limit, and a point total. You can do them in any order, unless that round says otherwise. You stay quiet in an individual round. In a team round you may talk, unless that round's note says not to. When you are done you close the booklet, say "finished," and keep your arm up until someone takes it. No headphones, no calculator.

Points come from a finished, correct puzzle. A wrong cell is not a partial, unless that round says otherwise. Finish every puzzle in the round and you get a bonus for each full minute left. The round header calls that bonus 10x or 7x, depending on the round. One small miss can still earn a reduced bonus, at the judge's discretion: 0.8 times the bonus, rounded to the closest integer, if at most two cells are wrong and they are in only one puzzle.

Team score is a formula, not a vibe. Add up each player's individual rounds, leave out the playoff, divide by 4, then add the team rounds. The booklet says that division is there so one player's individual points land in the same range as one team round.

A classic sudoku, in their words, is place 1 to 9 so each digit appears once in each row, column, and 3x3 box. That is the whole rule. Everything else in the booklet is that rule plus one more constraint: a diagonal, a cage sum, a path, a chess piece. Round 1 opens with a 25-point classic and then moves off into killer, irregular, diagonal, and the rest. Round 4, Acharya Drona, is nine classics and nothing else. They are worth 15, 25, 25, 30, 40, 65, 50, 30, and 70. The round is 35 minutes and 350 points, with a 7x time bonus. The booklet says those nine are there to test classic solving technique.

That is the round I recognize. The other rounds are variants. We don't run killer cages or diagonal constraints here. If you want the family tree of those extra rules, the [variants piece](/articles/sudoku-variants/) is the one. It is not a walkthrough of Kolkata.

## The last puzzle is an ordinary sudoku

The playoff is called The Final Duel. The top 8 on individual-round points go through. A tie on points is broken by the sum of their ranks across those rounds, and a tie after that is a random draw.

It is a knockout. Each match is 15 minutes and one submission. The winner is whoever hands in a correct grid first. If both are wrong, the one with more correct cells wins. If those counts match, the earlier hand-in wins. If that is still a tie, the higher seed wins.

The quarterfinals are variants: an Outside 234, a No Three in a Line, a Disguised Queen, and a Killer. Winning one is worth 100 points and a place in the semis. The semis are an Even sudoku and an Odd sudoku, 150 points each, solved on a 2 by 2 foot sheet on an easel, in front of the room. The final is a classic sudoku, 200 points, on the same kind of sheet.

Fifteen minutes, one submission, and a sheet the size of a coffee table. I would like to formally not.

Those playoff points get added to the individual-round total, and that sum is the championship. I don't know who won the final sheet. The published winning total is 6,959. I am not going to subtract 450 from it and pretend that means he swept the bracket. The booklet only says the points are added. It does not publish the match scores in the instruction pages, and the results feed was down when I looked.

## The classic part, on this site

Round 4 and the final are the same object we serve. A row, a column, a box, the digits 1 to 9 once each. The difference is the clock and the silence. I think the 70-point classic in that round is there to separate people who already do this fast.

[What makes a puzzle hard](/articles/what-makes-a-sudoku-hard/), here, is the hardest move the solver has to use. [How the levels are graded](/how-difficulty-works/) is the short version. Singles come first, a [naked single](/techniques/naked-single/) and then a [hidden single](/techniques/hidden-single/). Then [pointing](/techniques/pointing/) and [claiming](/techniques/claiming/). A Hard on this site has to need a [pair](/techniques/naked-pair/) or a [triple](/techniques/naked-triple/). Expert starts when the hardest move is a [quad](/techniques/quads/), an [X-wing](/techniques/x-wing/), an [XY-wing](/techniques/xy-wing/), or a [swordfish](/techniques/swordfish/). The same stretch of the solver also knows a [skyscraper](/techniques/skyscraper/), a [two-string kite](/techniques/two-string-kite/), and an [XYZ-wing](/techniques/xyz-wing/). [Coloring](/techniques/coloring/) and an [XY-chain](/techniques/xy-chain/) are the top band, Mental.

None of that is a claim about a Kolkata paper. I have not seen the competition booklets. The instruction booklet's examples are marked as not for commercial use, so I am not reprinting them. This grid is one of ours, an Expert, 26 clues. I ran the solver on it. It has one solution. The hardest technique it uses is an X-wing.

`...98..3..2......413.4....65...96..121........4.....9......74..4..13.7.2...5.4...`

```
+-------+-------+-------+
| . . . | 9 8 . | . 3 . |
| . 2 . | . . . | . . 4 |
| 1 3 . | 4 . . | . . 6 |
+-------+-------+-------+
| 5 . . | . 9 6 | . . 1 |
| 2 1 . | . . . | . . . |
| . 4 . | . . . | . 9 . |
+-------+-------+-------+
| . . . | . . 7 | 4 . . |
| 4 . . | 1 3 . | 7 . 2 |
| . . . | 5 . 4 | . . . |
+-------+-------+-------+
```

Singles and a couple of easier steps carry it. The move that sets the level is a rectangle of candidate 8s, not digits printed on the start grid: column 1 and column 9, lined up in row 6 and row 9. That is the [X-wing page](/techniques/x-wing/), and [practice for that move](/?practice=x-wing) if you want the drill instead of the essay. An [Expert](/?difficulty=expert) from the level list is the same band. A [Hard](/?difficulty=hard) stops lower, at pairs and triples. That is a fair Tuesday. It is not a 70-point classic with a minute bonus and a judge.

If you only learn one thing from Kolkata, learn the classic round before you chase the variants. The world title's last puzzle was a normal sudoku. The rest was extra rules on top of that. Start with a Hard. When the singles stop, the pair is the move. When a pair is no longer the hard part, the X-wing is the next page.

# Does Sudoku Actually Keep Your Brain Sharp?

I do a Sudoku almost every day. And at some point most of us who do have told ourselves a little story about it. This is good for me. It's exercise for the brain. It'll keep me sharp.

Does that story hold up? I went and read the actual studies instead of the headlines. Short version: it's more complicated, and more interesting, than "puzzles prevent dementia." Here's what I found, as honestly as I can put it.

*Quick note first: I run a puzzle site. I'm not a doctor, and nothing here is medical advice. If you're worried about your memory or thinking, talk to a healthcare professional.*

## The study everyone quotes

In 2019, researchers at the University of Exeter and King's College London published [a study of 19,078 healthy adults](https://doi.org/10.1002/gps.5085) aged 50 to 93, all part of an online research project called PROTECT [1]. Participants reported how often they did number puzzles like Sudoku, from never to more than once a day. Then they took a battery of online cognitive tests covering attention, reasoning, working memory and episodic memory.

The pattern was strong and consistent. People who did number puzzles more often scored better across all 14 measures, and the people who never did them scored notably worse than every other group [1].

A companion paper from the same project found a similar pattern for word puzzles. That's where the memorable headline came from. The university's [press release](https://news-archive.exeter.ac.uk/2019/may/title_716265_en.html) said word-puzzle users performed like people about ten years younger on a grammatical reasoning test [2]. Worth knowing if you see that number quoted about Sudoku...it was about crosswords and other word puzzles.

That's a big, careful dataset, and I don't want to wave it away. But look at what kind of study it is. Everyone was measured at one point in time. Nobody was assigned to do puzzles or not. So it can tell us that puzzle-doers and sharper thinkers are often the same people. It can't tell us which way the arrow points.

The authors were careful about this themselves. Their conclusion was that the relationship is close, and that further research should look at the type and difficulty of puzzles before treating them as an intervention [1].

## The arrow problem

"People who do puzzles are sharper" could happen at least three ways:

1. Puzzles sharpen your mind.
2. People with sharper minds enjoy puzzles more, so they do more of them. (When your thinking starts to slip, a Sudoku stops being fun.)
3. Something else, like education, income, health or general curiosity, drives both.

A [Scottish study published in the BMJ](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.k4925) in 2018 tried to untangle this. It followed 498 people born in 1936, who had taken an intelligence test as children in 1947, and tested them repeatedly in later life over about 15 years [3]. People who reported more intellectual engagement, especially problem solving, did have higher cognitive ability in late life. But engagement did not slow how fast their abilities declined.

The researchers' reading was that a mentally active life may raise your starting point, a kind of reserve, rather than change the slope of decline [3]. I find that genuinely encouraging. But notice it's not the same thing as "puzzles protect your brain."

## What happens when you actually assign people to train

The way around the arrow problem is a randomized trial. Split people into groups by chance, give one group the activity, compare.

The biggest and longest-running example is the [ACTIVE trial](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/195506) in the United States. It enrolled 2,832 adults aged 65 to 94 and randomly assigned them to memory training, reasoning training, speed-of-processing training, or no training, in about ten sessions [4]. Each kind of training improved the specific ability it targeted, and those gains lasted. But at two years, there were no significant effects on everyday functioning [4]. You got better at what you practiced.

That's the central finding of decades of "brain training" research, and it has a name: the transfer problem. Practice makes you better at the practiced task, sometimes at closely related tasks, and much less reliably at anything else.

In October 2014, the Stanford Center on Longevity and the Max Planck Institute for Human Development published [a statement](https://longevity.stanford.edu/a-consensus-on-the-brain-training-industry-from-the-scientific-community-2/) signed by a large group of psychologists and neuroscientists. They concluded that claims for brain games were "frequently exaggerated and at times misleading," and that "no studies have demonstrated that playing brain games cures or prevents Alzheimer's disease or other forms of dementia" [5].

That wasn't the end of it. In December 2014, a group of 127 scientists, organized by neuroscientist Michael Merzenich (a co-founder of the brain-training company Posit Science, a financial interest the signatory list flags), published [a rebuttal](https://www.cognitivetrainingdata.org/the-controversy-does-brain-training-work/response-letter/). They argued that some specific training programs do have peer-reviewed evidence of benefit, while agreeing that exaggerated marketing is a problem [6].

In 2016, a team led by Daniel Simons [reviewed the evidence](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100616661983) cited by brain-training companies. Training reliably improved the trained tasks. Evidence for closely related tasks was weaker, and evidence for distant tasks or everyday performance was very limited. Many studies had design problems [7]. The same year, the US Federal Trade Commission [got Lumosity's maker to pay $2 million](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/cases-proceedings/132-3212-lumos-labs-inc-lumosity-mobile-online-cognitive-game) to settle charges over advertising claims the agency said weren't backed by science [8].

## The ACTIVE twist

One result keeps the debate alive. In 2026, researchers [linked ACTIVE participants to their Medicare records](https://doi.org/10.1002/trc2.70197) over 20 years. People in the speed-training group who also completed at least one later "booster" session had a lower risk of a dementia diagnosis (a hazard ratio of 0.75). Speed training without boosters showed no difference, and memory and reasoning training showed no effect [9].

Intriguing, and it comes from a real randomized trial. But the details matter.

When [independent statisticians reviewed it](https://www.sciencemediacentre.org/expert-reaction-to-study-looking-at-cognitive-training-and-dementia/), they pointed out that the main randomized comparisons, each training group against the control group as a whole, did not show significant differences in dementia diagnoses. The positive result showed up in a subgroup of people who completed enough sessions to qualify for boosters, who may have been healthier to begin with, and there was no correction for testing several subgroups [10]. It was also a specific computerized speed task, not puzzles. A reason for more research. Not a reason to buy anything.

## What about Sudoku specifically?

Sudoku-specific trials exist, but they're small and early.

- A [2010 conference abstract](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0924-9338(10)70568-x) from Griffith University described a randomized trial of 30 healthy older adults. Two weeks of Sudoku practice improved scores on a reasoning test, and a further two weeks of harder puzzles improved them more, while the control group didn't change [11]. Thirty people, one reasoning test, published as an abstract. Interesting, not conclusive.
- A team at the University of Hong Kong ran a randomized trial of a 24-week Sudoku training program for 288 older adults with mild cognitive impairment. A [2023 conference abstract](https://doi.org/10.1093/geroni/igad104.2098) reported improvements in global cognition, working memory and memory recall compared with usual care [12]. The [trial registration](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04913857) has the full design if you're curious. That's the most substantial Sudoku study I found. But when I checked, the results were reported as an abstract, and the comparison group got usual care rather than a different engaging activity. So some of the benefit could come from attention, structure and social contact rather than Sudoku itself.

One more piece. A [2023 study of 10,318 Australians](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2023.23690) aged 70 and over found that people who more often did "active mental activities" like games, cards, chess, crosswords and puzzles had about a 9 percent lower risk of dementia over the study period [13]. Another observational result, same arrow problem, but it's consistent with the rest.

## So, does it keep your brain sharp?

Here's where I've landed, as someone who loves this puzzle and wants it to be good for me.

**Sudoku makes you better at Sudoku.** Well supported, and not nothing. You learn [techniques](/techniques/), you see patterns faster, you get more comfortable holding several possibilities in your head.

**People who do puzzles tend to think better.** That's consistent across big studies. Part of that is probably that sharp, curious people like puzzles. Part might be the puzzles. We don't know the split.

**There's no good evidence that Sudoku prevents dementia.** Nobody has shown that. Anyone selling it that way is ahead of the science.

**It might help a little, and it's unlikely to hurt.** A few small trials hint at modest benefits. The reserve idea from the Scottish study is plausible. And there's no downside to a daily habit you enjoy, as long as it isn't crowding out things with stronger evidence, like physical activity, sleep and time with people. The Stanford statement made the same point about opportunity cost [5].

If I had to guess at what matters most, I'd look at the [research on learning](/articles/why-being-stuck-is-good-for-you/), not brain games. The gains people see come from practicing something that's hard for them right now. Repeating puzzles you can already do on autopilot is relaxing, and that's a fine reason to do them. It's probably just not where any growth happens.

That's one of the reasons SudokuSudoku is built around a skill ladder: a puzzle that asks for exactly one technique you haven't mastered yet. Will it protect your brain? I can't promise that. I can promise it'll be more interesting. Start with a [Not Easy](/?difficulty=not-easy) and see where you stall.
